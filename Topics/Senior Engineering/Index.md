# Senior Engineering

Senior engineering is the practice of producing durable outcomes across a system and the people who build and operate it. The scope grows beyond completing a well-defined implementation: a senior engineer notices important problems, reduces ambiguity, makes decisions that hold up over time, and helps others deliver safely.

A senior title is not a checklist or a promise of authority. Expectations vary by organization. Use these guides as a framework for observable contribution, then compare them with the scope and values of the team you work in.

## Capability areas

- **Business and product judgment:** understand users, workflows, constraints, value, and the reason a change matters. See [[Topics/Business Analysis/Business Analysis and Solution Discovery]].
- **Technical judgment:** make proportionate architecture, data, security, reliability, and maintainability decisions. See [[Topics/Senior Engineering/Technical Judgment and Decision Making]] and [[Topics/Architecture and Senior Engineering]].
- **Execution through ambiguity:** frame a problem, make a plan, surface risks, deliver in increments, and use evidence to adapt. See [[Topics/Technical Leadership/Planning and Delivery]].
- **Communication and influence:** explain options and consequences to engineers, product partners, operators, and leaders; write decisions so people can act. See [[Topics/Senior Engineering/Communication and Influence]].
- **Leverage through others:** mentor, review, delegate, and improve shared engineering practices. See [[Topics/Technical Leadership/Team Leadership and Coaching]].
- **Ownership over the lifecycle:** carry a change from discovery and design through release, operations, learning, and follow-up.

## How senior impact becomes visible

Look for repeated evidence that your work improves the capability of a group, not only the output of one task. Examples include:

- A recurring user or operational problem is diagnosed and the team aligns on an outcome and a measurable baseline.
- An architectural decision is made at the right level, with constraints, alternatives, risks, and a way to revisit it.
- A risky migration or cross-team delivery is made easier to coordinate through explicit ownership and sequencing.
- A teammate takes on broader work because of coaching, useful review, or a clear decision record.
- A production issue produces a verified system or process improvement.
- A technical investment is explained in terms of future delivery cost, reliability, security, or customer impact.

Keep evidence factual: state the situation, your decisions and actions, who worked with you, the result, and how the result was measured. Distinguish delivered outcomes from intended benefits. [[Topics/Business Analysis/Leadership Evidence and Development]] maps the supplied CV to current strengths and development opportunities.

## Growth loop

1. Choose a real capability gap or recurring team problem.
2. Agree with your manager or peers what stronger performance would look like in your organization.
3. Take ownership of a bounded opportunity that requires that capability.
4. Ask affected people for feedback while the work is underway.
5. Record the decision, action, result, and evidence; include what did not work.
6. Review what changed for users or the team and select the next practice goal.
